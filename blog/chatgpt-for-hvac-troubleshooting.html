Can you use ChatGPT for HVAC troubleshooting?

Yes, for general questions. ChatGPT is good at explaining how a system works, decoding a term or talking through a fault tree, and many technicians already use it that way. What it cannot do on its own is know the unit in front of you: what failed on it before, what your colleague changed in April, what the controller logged last night. For that you need an assistant connected to your service records, which is what Kenner (getkenner.com) is.

Arthur Allilaire, CTO at Kenner10 November 2026 · 5 min read

In short

  • Use a general chatbot to learn, to have a concept explained and to think through a fault in general terms.
  • Do not rely on it for model-specific values without checking the manual or the nameplate.
  • It has not seen your work orders, your alarm logs or what was done on this unit before.
  • For daily service work, use an assistant that is connected to your own records and writes the job up.

Why technicians reach for it

Search for the best AI for HVAC technicians today and the top results are forum threads, such as this one on r/HVAC, where technicians recommend ChatGPT for making sense of manuals and concepts quickly. That makes sense. It is already on the phone, it costs little or nothing to try, and it explains things patiently at six in the morning when nobody at the office is picking up.

What a general chatbot is good at

  • Explaining concepts. Sequence of operation, superheat and subcooling, how an economizer decides what to do. A good way for a newer technician to learn.
  • Working through a manual you give it. Upload or paste a section and ask what it means in plain words.
  • Thinking out loud. Listing the usual causes of a symptom so you can rule them out in order.
  • Writing. Turning rough notes into a tidy message for a customer.

Where it falls short on a service call

It does not know this unit

The most useful facts on a call are local: this case iced up twice last month, the last visit replaced the fan motor, the store manager says it started after the cleaning crew came. A general chatbot has none of that unless someone types it in, on every job.

It can be confidently wrong

OpenAI says so itself: in its help center it writes that ChatGPT “can produce incorrect or misleading outputs” and advises users to verify technical information. On a service call the risky answers are the specific ones, such as a part number, a charge amount or a terminal on a wiring diagram.

Bluon, which sells an HVAC-R equipment database, publishes its own comparison: in its tests, generic AI with web search answered 75% of tech support questions correctly, and only 11% of replacement part questions. Those are a vendor’s figures about its competitors, not an independent study, so read them as that. The pattern they describe is the one to watch for: fine on theory, shaky on the exact part.

Nothing gets written down

The conversation stays in one person’s chat history. The report still has to be written, the refrigerant record still has to be filled in, and the next technician at that unit sees none of what was learned.

Your customer’s data ends up in a personal account

Site names, addresses and equipment details pasted into a personal chatbot account are outside anything your company has agreed with its customers. Check your own policy before that becomes a habit across the team.

A sensible way to use both

These are different tools for different questions. Let technicians keep a general chatbot as a tutor, and agree three rules for it:

  1. Give it the exact model number, and ask it which manual page an answer comes from.
  2. Check any value you are about to act on against the manual or the nameplate.
  3. Keep customer and site details out of personal accounts.

Common questions

Is ChatGPT accurate for HVAC troubleshooting?

Often for general theory, less reliably for model-specific values such as part numbers, charge amounts or wiring. OpenAI itself advises verifying technical information. Check anything specific to a model against the manual or the nameplate.

What is the best AI for HVAC technicians?

It depends on the question. For explanations and learning, a general chatbot is fine. For model data and parts across brands, use a tool built on an equipment database. For a commercial service team that wants answers from its own units’ history and the report written afterwards, use an assistant connected to your service records, such as Kenner.

Can ChatGPT read our work orders?

Not on its own. It works from general knowledge, web search and whatever is pasted or uploaded into the conversation, so someone has to hand it the history on every job.

Should our technicians stop using ChatGPT?

No. It is a good tutor. Agree what may be pasted into it, treat model-specific answers as something to verify, and give technicians a connected assistant for the questions that depend on your own records.
